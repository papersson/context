## Failure modes of interconnected knowledge bases maintained by small teams

A consistent theme across enterprise-wiki research is that *interconnection and structure create long-term fragility unless maintenance is funded as a first-class activity*. In an OpenSym field study spanning multiple organisations, researchers had access to usage data from “thousands of wikis” and then layered surveys and interviews on top. They report a “high wiki mortality rate” and give a quantified indicator: **66%** of respondents said their wiki **had not been used in the past six months**. citeturn16view2 Crucially for your flat-vs-wiki debate, the study argues that the problem is not merely “having too little structure” but that **early organisation choices** (page naming, content grouping) become effectively locked-in: reorganising later was described as *daunting* due to technical constraints, the volume of information, and disagreement over what constitutes “better” structure. citeturn15view3turn16view2

The same study links structural debt to cross-references. Arbitrary or underspecified page names that looked fine initially later **hindered search and meaningful reorganisation—especially once links had been established**. citeturn15view3 It also highlights a maintenance failure mode that directly maps to link-rot dynamics: mature wikis accumulate **stale content** that does not “evolve in step” with organisational change, and participants reported that **pruning stale content was not something they engaged in**. citeturn15view3 While this is not a direct measurement of “broken internal links”, it is a well-documented operational analogue: the biggest burden is *keeping a living graph coherent under change* when “nobody has time”.

On adoption and contributor behaviour, an enterprise case study (CHI extended abstract) identifies cost-related inhibitors that disproportionately affect part-time maintainers: staff were reluctant to share due to perceived **extra cost**, sensitivity about “unfinished” or “sub-par” work, lack of guidelines, and cultural concerns around editing others’ content. citeturn14view0 This connects to the small-team setting: any design that increases authoring friction (e.g., mandatory link typing, strict taxonomies, required relationship assertions) competes with contributors’ limited time. A separate enterprise-wiki survey paper synthesises prior literature and explicitly warns that *poor structural support* leads to **labourious insertion/retrieval, navigation problems, and duplication**, and argues for a **balance** between wiki looseness and strict schemas. citeturn6view0 That “balance” is, in practice, the central sustainability problem you are facing.

## How filesystem-searching LLM agents perform with flat vs hierarchical or graph-structured corpora

Direct, head-to-head benchmark literature that varies *knowledge-base authoring topology* (flat Markdown collection vs wiki-link graph vs typed-taxonomy KB) for *tool-using agents with grep/glob* is still thin as of early 2026. Instead, the closest empirical evidence comes from adjacent settings where agents (or agent-like retrieval loops) must localise relevant context in large file trees and then reason over it, and where retrieval is intentionally lexical. citeturn17view2turn20view0turn19view0 The practical implication is that we can support some design principles (what helps lexical localisation) more confidently than we can support a categorical verdict that “wikis outperform flat docs” or vice versa.

In a 2026 study on grep-like retrieval for repository-level code completion, the authors show that an LLM generating ripgrep queries (“Naive GrepRAG”) can achieve **performance comparable to—and in parts outperform—complex graph-based baselines**, because it retrieves fragments that are lexically precise and near the completion site. citeturn17view2 They also identify failure modes that are portable to your KB: (a) **keyword ambiguity** (high-frequency terms produce noisy matches) and (b) **context fragmentation/redundancy** (overlapping hits truncated and presented out of logical order). citeturn17view2 This suggests that, for grep-based agents, “graph structure” is not automatically beneficial; what matters is whether the corpus exposes **high-precision lexical anchors** (unique identifiers, stable names, constrained terminology) and whether the agent can assemble enough contiguous context to reason.

A second line of evidence examines file-native “context engineering” more directly. A 2026 experimental study evaluates agents reading structured knowledge via file tools (grep/read) across thousands of runs. It finds file-based multi-file retrieval gives frontier models a small but statistically significant accuracy gain (+2.7%, p=0.029) and that **domain-partitioned** schema layouts scale to very large schemas while maintaining navigation accuracy; it also introduces the “grep tax”, where compact/novel formats can increase runtime tokens because grep outputs become dense or models use search patterns poorly. citeturn20view0 Although this is not a flat-vs-wiki comparison, it is evidence that **hierarchical partitioning and predictably grep-able structure** can improve multi-step agent behaviour—without needing a wiki-style link graph.

Finally, for document QA, a 2025/2026 study reports that tool-augmented agents using only keyword search can reach **over 90%** of a vector-RAG baseline’s metrics while reducing ongoing maintenance burdens, framing this as suitable when knowledge bases update frequently. citeturn19view2 In a separate “deep research” retrieval benchmark, BM25 beats LLM-based retrievers by ~30% because agents generate keyword-oriented sub-queries; augmenting documents with metadata/keywords improved performance. citeturn19view1 Taken together, these results imply a conservative conclusion for your situation: if your agents retrieve primarily via grep and keyword search, **designing for lexical retrievability and predictable file navigation is evidence-backed**, while the incremental benefit of heavy cross-link graphs is not yet robustly established in agent benchmarks.

## State of the art in agent-native knowledge base design for grep and selective reading

The 2025–2026 literature increasingly frames “agent-friendly” design as *context engineering*: structuring persistent external knowledge so that agents can efficiently locate and read the right slices using file operations. citeturn20view0turn5search28 A key empirical result is that **format choice alone** (YAML vs JSON vs Markdown in the tested setup) does not reliably change accuracy overall, but that **partitioning** and “grep-ability” does matter for efficiency due to the “grep tax”—dense outputs and unfamiliar patterns lead to more tool calls and token use. citeturn20view0turn19view0 This pushes design attention away from “pick the perfect markup” and toward: stable naming, consistent headings, predictable section layouts, and domain-partitioned files.

On what measurably improves retrieval *for keyword-oriented agents*, the best-supported “mechanistic” findings are about lexical anchors and disambiguation. The grep-retrieval study shows that retrieval is effective when queries are based on high-signal identifiers (in code, class/method/variable names) and breaks down under ambiguous or generic keywords. citeturn17view2 The deep-research benchmark similarly shows agents tend to issue keyword-oriented sub-queries, which is why BM25 (surface-form matching) outperforms LLM-based retrievers in that setting. citeturn19view1 For your KB, the parallel is straightforward: message-type names, field names, rule identifiers, and canonical business terms should be first-class lexical anchors (appearing in headings, “AKA/aliases” sections, and in examples), and ambiguous “common language” headings should be treated as navigational liabilities unless paired with disambiguators.

A modern caution is emerging around “agent guidance” files (analogous to a top-level KB “how to use this” doc). A 2026 study finds repository context files (AGENTS.md-style) **reduced task success rates** and increased inference cost by >20%, concluding that unnecessary requirements can make tasks harder and that human-written context should be minimal. citeturn21view1 Yet another 2026 empirical study finds AGENTS.md presence is associated with **lower runtime (Δ28.64%) and reduced token consumption (Δ16.58%)** for coding agents while maintaining comparable completion behaviour. citeturn22search5 The transferable point is not “always add a massive agent primer” but “treat agent-facing scaffolding as a hypothesis and measure it”: too much context can induce broader exploration and cost, but the right constraints can speed up work. citeturn21view1turn22search5

## Migration patterns from flat Markdown to more structured forms

The enterprise-wiki sustainability literature includes an implicit migration story: teams often start with ad hoc pages, then discover that early decisions and link webs become difficult to refactor. The OpenSym study explicitly says early organisation choices can have “profound downstream impacts”, and that reorganising later is daunting; stale content accumulates and is hard to manage. citeturn15view3turn16view2 This supports a design approach where the initial state is intentionally simple **but** future refactors are anticipated by reserving durable identifiers (stable page/file names; stable concept IDs), and by designing link conventions that can be linted or rewritten at scale.

On how to evolve toward more semantics without killing participation, a wiki-project-management paper argues that adding semantic metadata works only if **granularity is chosen carefully** (it proposes two broad “axes” of metadata), and emphasises that semantic additions should remain **optional** because raising the entry barrier undermines the “low barrier” property that makes wiki workflows viable. citeturn9view0 It also describes “light constraints”: validators that warn about missing structure or missing explanations (e.g., first-time acronyms) without blocking publication, shifting enforcement away from manual “wiki gnomes”. citeturn9view0 This is a concrete migration pattern: start with flat pages, add optional metadata, then add non-blocking validation, and only later enforce stricter schema-aware constraints when the social process is stable.

For evolving from linked-text KBs to explicit knowledge graphs, the semantic web literature provides a warning: typed relationships introduce an ongoing QA burden. Research on entity["organization","Wikidata","collaborative knowledge base"] formalises its constraint system and analyses the prevalence/evolution of constraint violations, and later work taxonomises repair strategies (including schema-level repairs). citeturn2search11turn2search15turn2search19 This suggests clear prerequisites for “flat → KG” success: a stable vocabulary, a constraint/validation layer, and an explicit repair workflow. Complementarily, the extraction literature around entity["organization","DBpedia","semantic web project"] shows that keeping structured extractions synchronised with evolving source content can require “live” pipelines precisely because heavyweight release processes lag behind changes. citeturn10search10 For a small team, the constraint/repair requirement is the main predictor of whether a structured graph approach will remain sustainable.

## Trade-offs between self-contained redundancy and DRY atomicity for humans and grep-based agents

For humans, there is strong evidence that highly interlinked, non-linear structures can increase navigation difficulties. A controlled experiment comparing linear text with hypertext topologies reports empirical disorientation/navigation performance effects linked to topology choices. citeturn7search0 Review work also documents that some studies find navigation problems (uncertainty about what has been read and where to go next; cognitive overload), while others find mixed or null effects—i.e., the cost is context-dependent and relates to cues and scaffolding. citeturn7search16 This matters for “atomic/DRY” architectures: when information is decomposed into many small nodes, the reader’s job becomes pathfinding, not comprehension, unless the system supplies strong orientation aids.

Large-scale wiki practice illustrates that “controlled redundancy” can be a feature, not a bug. Wikipedia’s editorial “summary style” explicitly recommends that subtopic articles remain standalone and include enough broader context, **even if this produces duplication** between parent and child pages. citeturn7search7 Interpreted for your KB: some duplication can be an intentional trade—supporting standalone comprehension, reducing link-chasing, and giving both humans and agents enough local context to interpret rules correctly—while still maintaining a canonical “source of truth” for normative definitions.

For grep-based LLM agents, the evidence suggests a different but related trade-off: redundancy can improve recall by repeating key lexical anchors, but it can also increase noise and ambiguity. The grep-retrieval study shows keyword ambiguity is a primary failure mode and that fragmentation/redundant snippets degrade model comprehension. citeturn17view2 The practical implication is that “DRY atomic notes” are not automatically optimal for agents: if decomposition causes fragmentation (critical preconditions split across many files without repeated anchor terms), agents may fail to assemble the needed context. Conversely, repeated generic phrasing across many docs can cause ambiguous grep hits. The evidence supports a hybrid: keep *normative definitions* centralised, but allow *contextual restatement* (short, structured summaries) in downstream docs to improve both human comprehension and agent localisation. citeturn7search7turn17view2

## Concrete recommendations supported by the evidence

Adopt a “flat-first with reserved structure” approach: start with self-contained Markdown documents, but treat file names and headings as long-lived identifiers. This is supported by evidence that early content organisation choices become hard to change later and that reorganising mature linked corpora is daunting. citeturn15view3turn16view2 “Flat-first” reduces early cross-reference debt, while “reserved structure” (stable IDs) avoids painting yourselves into a corner once links/taxonomies become necessary.

Optimise first for lexical retrievability (grep/BM25-style), not for hyperlink density. Empirical results in multiple agent settings show keyword-oriented query generation is common, and lexical retrieval can be surprisingly strong when identifiers are distinctive; failure modes are ambiguity and fragmentation. citeturn17view2turn19view1turn19view2 Concretely, that implies: every concept page should expose canonical names (message type / rule ID), include common aliases/synonyms, and use predictable headings (“Definition”, “Constraints”, “Edge cases”, “Examples”, “Related message types”) so agents can pattern-match and selectively read.

Use hierarchical folders for *partitioning* and “information scent”, but avoid governance-heavy taxonomies until contributor workflows are stable. The enterprise wiki evidence shows “content organisation and flexibility” is a long-term challenge and that early structural decisions can create downstream constraints. citeturn15view3turn6view0 Meanwhile, file-native agent experiments show multi-file partitioning can scale and may modestly improve accuracy for frontier models, while overly compact or unfamiliar formats can create a “grep tax.” citeturn20view0 The evidence-backed middle ground is: a shallow directory layout aligned to stable domains (e.g., “messages/”, “rules/”, “patterns/”, “faq/”), plus per-domain indexes, not a fully normalised ontology on day one.

Introduce links and typed relationships incrementally, and make them optional at first. Research on adding semantic data to wikis argues that raising the entry barrier discourages participation; it recommends carefully chosen metadata granularity and “light constraints” that warn rather than block. citeturn9view0 Combined with enterprise-wiki findings that wikis require ongoing “gardening” and suffer when maintenance is unfunded, the supported strategy is phased: (a) optional link blocks (“Related: …”), (b) optional typed relation stubs (“rel: is-parent-of”), (c) non-blocking validation, then (d) stricter enforcement once the team can sustain it.

Bake in “gardening” as an explicit rotating responsibility, with automation focused on detecting drift rather than enforcing perfect structure. Enterprise wiki research describes that stale content accumulates and that users do not routinely prune it, despite recognising confusion and resource costs. citeturn15view3turn16view2 Link-rot evidence from adjacent contexts shows links decay and are rarely updated while targets evolve. citeturn0academia33 For your KB, the sustainable pattern is: light automation (broken-link detection, orphan detection, “recently changed core docs” dashboards) plus a human gardening cadence that prioritises high-traffic/high-risk pages.

Design for “controlled redundancy”: enforce a canonical definition, but allow local summaries where the concept is used. Wikipedia practice explicitly allows duplication to keep pages standalone. citeturn7search7 Hypertext research shows navigation cost can rise in highly non-linear structures. citeturn7search0turn7search16 The agent retrieval evidence shows fragmentation and ambiguity are failure modes. citeturn17view2 The combined recommendation is: keep one canonical “Definition/Spec” section per concept, but require that downstream pages include a short, standardised “Local summary” (1–3 sentences + key identifiers) so both humans and agents do not have to chase links for basic context.

Treat “agent guidance” documents as experimental artefacts and measure their effect. Two 2026 empirical lines conflict: one finds AGENTS.md-like context files reduce success and raise cost, another finds improved runtime/tokens with comparable completion behaviour. citeturn21view1turn22search5 This supports a conservative approach for your KB: keep any global “Agent entrypoint” file minimal (directory map, naming conventions, where canonical definitions live), avoid large policy essays, and validate with your own agent evaluation before institutionalising it.

Delay full knowledge-graph commitments until you have the capacity for constraint and repair workflows. Evidence from Wikidata constraint research shows that typed relations at scale require formal constraints, ongoing violation detection, and repair strategies (including schema-level repairs). citeturn2search11turn2search15turn2search19 If your end-state includes KG construction, the evidence suggests you should start by capturing *graph-ready signals* in text (stable IDs, typed “relation” stubs, explicit cardinalities) rather than maintaining a strict graph from day one.

## Open questions that the literature does not settle

There is no widely accepted benchmark that directly varies *KB topology* (flat Markdown vs dense wiki link graph vs typed relationship KB) while holding the retrieval tools fixed to grep/glob and measuring tool-using agent success, cost, and failure modes. Existing evidence is mostly adjacent (code-repo retrieval; file-native schema navigation; document QA). citeturn17view2turn20view0turn19view2

Quantified data on “link rot” and “reference drift” inside enterprise knowledge bases specifically (internal links, section anchors, renamed pages) is surprisingly limited in the open literature; the best quantitative proxies are from adjacent ecosystems (e.g., link decay in code comments) and large public wikis. citeturn0academia33turn16view2

Empirical thresholds for when taxonomy/typed links “pay off” (e.g., document count, contributor count, change rate) are not well established. The enterprise wiki study shows early choices matter and reorganising is costly, but it does not yield a clean quantitative tipping point for “now you need an ontology.” citeturn15view3turn16view2turn6view0

The optimal level and placement of redundancy for grep-based agents (how much repeated context improves recall vs how much it increases ambiguity/noise) is not parameterised in current agent benchmarks; the evidence identifies ambiguity and fragmentation as failure modes, but does not prescribe a measurable “redundancy budget.” citeturn17view2turn19view1

How to generalise conflicting findings on repository “context files” to domain knowledge bases remains unresolved: one line of work finds success-rate harm and cost increase, another finds efficiency gains. The boundary conditions (content style, constraint strictness, task distribution, agent design) need controlled experiments in KB-like domains. citeturn21view1turn22search5
