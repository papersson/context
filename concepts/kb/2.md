
# Designing knowledge bases for humans and LLM agents: what the evidence says

**The strongest finding across all five research questions is this: start flat, make documents self-contained, and add structure only when retrieval pain demands it.** Empirical evidence from hypertext cognition research, LLM retrieval benchmarks, corporate wiki post-mortems, and information retrieval studies converges on a "write DRY, publish self-contained" architecture with deferred structuring. The literature also reveals a critical gap: no controlled study directly compares flat vs. structured knowledge bases for LLM filesystem agents, forcing practitioners to triangulate from adjacent research in RAG evaluation, coding agent benchmarks, and corporate knowledge management.

## Q1: Interconnected knowledge bases fail predictably for small teams

The quantitative evidence on structural decay in knowledge bases is sobering. Pew Research Center's analysis of 1 million web pages found **25% became inaccessible within a decade**, with even 2-year-old pages showing ~20% loss. Within controlled environments like digital libraries, Nelson & Allen (2002) found slower decay at ~3% per year. The most striking structural finding comes from EPFL and Wikimedia's analysis of 319 Wikipedia language editions: **15% of all articles are orphans** with no incoming links, effectively invisible to navigating readers. Wikipedia's de-orphanization rate of 0.5% per month means that at current rates, clearing the backlog would take 20 years—and this is with the largest volunteer editing force in history plus automated bots. For a 5–8 person team without such infrastructure, content creation will inevitably outpace structural integration.

The maintenance burden compounds nonlinearly. Zettelkasten.de's practitioners describe an "Evernote effect" where maintenance effort grows superlinearly with entries—eventually exceeding the value returned. Enterprise knowledge management literature quantifies the broader failure pattern: **50–70% of KM system projects fail**, primarily from cultural and adoption issues rather than technology problems. A survey of 500 engineers found **78% cite outdated information** as the reason they distrust internal documentation, and 84% of developers prefer asking colleagues over consulting wikis. This creates a vicious cycle: once a critical mass of users encounters stale content, trust collapses, usage drops, contributions decline, and staleness accelerates. Taxonomy drift compounds the problem—the APQC Process Classification Framework showed significant structural reorganization across versions, and without a dedicated taxonomy steward (which small teams cannot afford), classification inconsistency accumulates progressively.

The evidence also reveals that even thriving enterprise wikis are a minority. A WikiSym study found that among thousands of enterprise wikis examined, "thriving wikis were a minority." IBM's internal wiki studies (CHI 2010) identified that while users perceive wikis as valuable, the primary motivation for contribution is enjoyment—not obligation—making sustained contribution fragile. For a small team, the implication is clear: an interconnected knowledge base with wikilinks, taxonomies, and typed relationships represents a significant maintenance liability. The team should expect **10–15% of content to become functionally invisible** within 1–2 years without active link maintenance, and should plan for **10–20% of team capacity** dedicated to KB upkeep—equivalent to roughly one person's half-time effort on a team of eight.

## Q2: Flat vs. structured retrieval depends on query complexity

The most important finding in this area is negative: **no published study directly compares LLM agent filesystem retrieval across different KB organizational structures** in a controlled experiment. All available evidence comes from coding agent benchmarks (where "KB" means codebase), RAG pipeline comparisons, or practitioner reports. That said, the triangulated evidence draws a nuanced picture.

For single-hop fact retrieval, flat file collections with filesystem tools perform remarkably well. Letta Research's benchmark found that an agent using grep and semantic search over flat files achieved **74.0% on the LoCoMo QA benchmark**, beating Mem0's knowledge-graph variant at 68.5%. Anthropic's Claude Code team arrived at a similar conclusion empirically: they tested RAG with Voyage embeddings against agentic grep/glob search and found the latter "outperformed everything, by a lot." The key mechanism is iterative refinement—agents autonomously reformulate queries across multiple search rounds, compensating for flat structure through persistence. Simple filesystem tools that appear heavily in training data (grep, glob, cat) are used more effectively by models than specialized retrieval APIs.

However, this advantage reverses for multi-hop reasoning. On SWE-bench Pro (multi-file coding tasks averaging 4.1 files changed), Augment Code's semantic indexing achieved **51.8% resolution** compared to lower scores from grep-based agents using the same underlying model. As Augment's analysis noted: "Text-based search tools like grep find the top-level APIs easily, but most agents stopped there." Aider's graph-based approach provides the clearest efficiency argument for structure: its dependency graph with PageRank ranking uses **8,500–13,000 tokens** per task compared to Claude Code's **108,000–117,000 tokens**—roughly a 10× difference in context consumption. StructRAG's benchmark results reinforce this pattern: converting documents to structured formats (tables, graphs, catalogs) improved LLM reasoning scores by **9–23 points**, with benefits growing as document length increases. The NeurIPS 2024 SWE-agent paper provides the strongest causal evidence: custom agent-computer interface tools with structured output improved resolution rates by **10.7 percentage points** over raw shell—demonstrating that how information is surfaced matters more than raw storage format.

The synthesis is clear: **for a knowledge base of hundreds of interrelated documents, flat structure will work initially for simple lookups, but will increasingly fail as agents need to resolve cross-document relationships.** The path forward is not to choose between flat and structured, but to maintain a lightweight structural layer (index files, cross-reference metadata, topic maps) that helps agents navigate without requiring full graph traversal.

## Q3: LLM-friendly document design has strong empirical foundations

Document formatting is not cosmetic—it measurably determines LLM comprehension. Braun et al.'s controlled study on the CUAD legal benchmark found that **well-structured Markdown improved GPT-4.1 accuracy by approximately 20 percentage points**, with an additional 10–13 pp gain from "meta-cognitive priming" (telling the model it will receive structured Markdown). He et al.'s systematic comparison across four GPT models and six benchmarks found **up to 40% performance variation** from format choice alone, with Markdown generally optimal for GPT-4 class models. For tabular data specifically, Markdown key-value format achieved **60.7% accuracy** versus CSV at 44.3%—a 16-point gap on identical content.

The "lost in the middle" effect, documented by Liu et al. in TACL 2024, establishes that LLMs retrieve information worst from the middle of their context window, following a U-shaped performance curve. This has direct implications for document design: **critical information should appear at the beginnings and ends of sections**, and retrieval systems should order documents strategically. The effect becomes more pronounced with 20–30 documents and persists even in explicitly long-context models. A complementary study from November 2025 found that spatial retrieval capability degrades faster than semantic comprehension as context grows, and that "model performance is significantly governed by the format of the input data, even when the content is semantically identical."

For chunking and section design, the evidence converges on a sweet spot of **400–1,000 tokens per section** with clear topical boundaries. A February 2026 systematic evaluation found that simple paragraph-based chunking achieved the highest nDCG@10 scores across four embedding models, outperforming more complex semantic and LLM-based splitting methods. Anthropic's Contextual Retrieval work demonstrated that making each chunk self-describing (prepending a brief contextual summary) reduces retrieval failure by **35–67%**. The emerging practitioner consensus from RAG platform operators like Kapa.ai identifies several concrete anti-patterns: contextual dependencies across sections (the #1 problem), semantic discoverability gaps (if a feature name isn't in a chunk, it won't be retrieved), and implicit knowledge assumptions. The practical advice: every section should include explicit product/feature names, state what it's about, and include enough context to stand alone.

The llms.txt specification (by Jeremy Howard) proposes a standardized index file at `/llms.txt` with ~951 domain adoptions as of mid-2025, but **no empirical study has evaluated its effectiveness** on agent retrieval. GitHub's analysis of 2,500+ repositories with `agents.md` files found that effective agent instruction files are concise, example-rich, and describe capabilities rather than file paths—because domain concepts are more stable than directory structure. The AGENTS.md research also suggests an **instruction budget of approximately 150–200 rules** that frontier models can reliably follow, arguing for progressive disclosure rather than monolithic instruction documents.

## Q4: Successful migrations start flat and add structure from pain

The GitLab handbook provides the strongest longitudinal evidence of successful organic migration. Starting with **~10 employees in 2015** writing simple markdown files in a Git repository, it grew to 605,000 words across 550 pages by 2019 (at ~550 employees) and exceeded 7,000 printed pages by 2025. The critical insight from GitLab's experience is that structure emerged from team growth, not from top-down taxonomy design. The cultural norm—"whenever someone asks a question in Slack, someone answers with a link to the handbook"—was established early and scaled naturally. GitLab's authors explicitly assessed at 550 employees that "the handbook works as well for us now as it did with 100 employees."

The "premature architecture" antipattern is the most consistently documented failure mode. An Obsidian community analysis of beginner failure patterns identified three recurring problems: premature architecture (building elaborate folder/tag systems before accumulating meaningful content), borrowed workflows (replicating someone else's system without understanding why it works), and plugin escalation (responding to friction with more complexity). All three share what the analysis calls a "structural inversion: beginners optimize for sophistication instead of reducing capture friction." Martin Fowler's YAGNI principle, backed by Kohavi et al.'s empirical finding that **⅔ of speculatively built features at Microsoft were never needed**, provides the strongest theoretical grounding: the "cost of carry" of premature structure—making every new document harder to create because the author must decide where it fits—is a real and recurring tax. Steph Ango (Obsidian CEO) reports keeping most notes in the vault root, organized by minimal metadata properties rather than folders, with structure emerging from heavy internal linking. Christian Tietze, a Zettelkasten practitioner of 15+ years, reports having "ditched any kind of classification" after several years because it "turned out to be just artificial post-hoc naming and not related to any productive action."

The Diátaxis framework (tutorials, how-to guides, reference, explanation) provides the most evidence-backed incremental structuring approach. Sequin's case study demonstrates a small engineering team successfully restructuring existing docs using Diátaxis, with the concrete technique of creating "phantom links" to nonexistent reference pages to naturally map what reference docs were needed. Canonical adopted Diátaxis across multiple documentation properties, noting that "the first thing Diátaxis does is make existing documentation look worse, not better"—a feature, not a bug, because it makes structural problems visible. The documentation maturity model literature (Hackos 2017, Huang & Tilley 2003) suggests a five-level progression from ad hoc to optimized, with the critical insight that a small team should target Level 2 (Repeatable—consistent processes exist) before attempting Level 3 (Defined—standardized processes). Premature formalization precedes the pain that would justify it.

## Q5: The evidence resolves the self-contained vs. atomic debate

The strongest finding in the entire research is that the self-contained vs. atomic framing is a **false dichotomy**. The optimal approach, supported by multiple independent lines of evidence, is **atomic AND self-contained**—achieved through decontextualization.

For LLM retrieval, the evidence is unambiguous. Chen et al.'s "Dense X Retrieval" (EMNLP 2024) introduced "propositions"—defined as "atomic expressions within text, each encapsulating a distinct factoid and presented in a concise, self-contained natural language format"—and demonstrated they **significantly outperform passage-level retrieval** units. Anthropic's Contextual Retrieval work showed that making chunks self-contained by prepending explanatory context reduced retrieval failure by **49%** (and 67% with reranking). A clinical RAG study found self-contained adaptive chunks achieved **87% accuracy** versus 50% for context-dependent fixed-size chunks (p = 0.001). Choi et al.'s foundational "Decontextualization" paper (TACL 2021, Google Research) demonstrated that rewriting sentences to stand alone while preserving meaning improved both human usability and retrieval efficiency—the bridge between atomic and self-contained.

For human comprehension, DeStefano and LeFevre's meta-review of **38 empirical studies** found that hypertext with embedded links increases cognitive load, with the effect scaling with link density. However, Zumbach and Mohraz's controlled experiment revealed a critical nuance: for **encyclopedic/reference content, non-linear linked presentation does NOT harm comprehension**—the cognitive overhead applies primarily to narrative and sequential content like tutorials. This means linked, atomic documentation is acceptable for reference material (which constitutes most of a domain knowledge base) while self-contained documents are better for procedural and tutorial content.

The evidence against naive redundancy is equally strong. Bernstein and Zobel's CIKM 2005 analysis found that **16.6% of relevant documents** in TREC runs were content-redundant, causing up to **39% degradation in nDCG** scores. Multiple RAG studies demonstrate that redundant passages waste context window slots and can change correct answers to incorrect ones—with filtering "detrimental" passages improving accuracy by **10%** on Natural Questions and TriviaQA. The practical synthesis: **author and maintain content in DRY format** (single canonical definition of each concept) to prevent inconsistency, but **serve and index content in self-contained format** where each chunk includes enough context to be understood independently. This is exactly what Anthropic's Contextual Retrieval implements: atomic chunks plus prepended context equals self-contained atomic units. For a team using flat markdown files, this means each document should define its concepts once but include a brief contextual header stating what the document covers, which domain it relates to, and what terminology it uses—making it independently comprehensible without requiring the reader (human or LLM) to follow links.

## What the evidence concretely supports for this team

Based on the convergent findings above, the strongest evidence-backed recommendations for a 5–8 person team with part-time contributors building a knowledge base for hundreds of interrelated proto message types and business rules are:

**Phase 1 (Months 1–3): Flat markdown with self-contained documents.** Start with a flat collection of well-structured markdown files. Each document should cover one topic, use descriptive filenames with consistent naming conventions, include a YAML front matter block (title, brief description, key terms, last-verified date), and be independently comprehensible without following any links. Use clear heading hierarchies and place critical information at the beginning of each section. Target **400–1,000 tokens per major section**. Establish a cultural norm of "write it down, link to it in chat" (the GitLab pattern). Do not build a taxonomy, folder hierarchy, or typed relationship system yet. Use liberal unresolved internal links (like `[[concept-name]]`) as breadcrumbs for future connections, but do not require them to be followed for comprehension.

**Phase 2 (Months 3–6): Add lightweight navigation layers when search fails.** When team members report inability to find documents, add index files—lightweight maps of content (analogous to llms.txt) that list related documents by topic area. These serve as the progressive disclosure layer for both humans and agents. Create these bottom-up from observed search patterns, not top-down from anticipated taxonomy. Add a simple tagging system only if search consistently fails, and constrain it to a short controlled vocabulary reviewed quarterly.

**Phase 3 (Months 6–12): Introduce Diátaxis-style content typing.** Separate documents into reference (proto message type definitions, business rules), how-to guides (common operations), and explanation (architectural context, design rationale). This separation improves both human navigation and LLM retrieval by creating topically coherent chunks. Begin periodic "knowledge refactoring" sessions—quarterly reviews where the team collectively restructures problem areas, resolves orphaned content, and updates stale documents.

**Phase 4 (12+ months): Consider structural enrichment for multi-hop queries.** Only when agents and humans consistently need to trace relationships across multiple documents (e.g., "which business rules affect this proto field across all downstream services?") should the team invest in explicit cross-reference graphs or typed relationships. The Aider model—building a dependency graph from existing references and using PageRank to surface relevant documents—provides 10× token efficiency over brute-force search and can be constructed automatically from existing internal links.

Three cross-cutting practices supported by evidence:

- **Automate link checking.** This is the single most evidence-backed maintenance intervention. Internal link rot of ~3%/year is achievable with good practices; without monitoring, expect 10–20% link breakage within 2 years.
- **Track freshness metadata.** Include a `last-verified` date in each document's front matter. Once more than ~30% of documents are perceived as stale, trust collapses and the knowledge base enters a death spiral. Automated staleness alerts are cheap insurance.
- **Budget 10–20% of team capacity for maintenance.** This is the empirically-grounded cost of keeping a knowledge base alive. Without this investment, the 50–70% KMS failure rate applies.

## What the literature does not answer

Several critical questions for this team's context remain empirically unresolved:

**No controlled study compares LLM agent retrieval across KB organizational structures.** All evidence is triangulated from coding benchmarks, RAG evaluations, and practitioner reports. A direct comparison—flat markdown vs. wikilinked markdown vs. knowledge graph, same content, same agent tools, same queries—does not exist. This is the single largest gap.

**No empirical data on the relationship between cross-reference density and maintenance cost.** Practitioners consistently report that maintenance grows with interconnectedness, but no study quantifies the function—is it linear, quadratic, or worse? The "Evernote effect" describes superlinear growth but provides no measurements.

**No study evaluates YAML front matter vs. no front matter for LLM agent retrieval.** Practitioner consensus strongly favors structured metadata, but no controlled experiment measures the marginal retrieval improvement.

**The optimal document granularity for agent filesystem retrieval is unknown.** Should a proto message type and all its business rules be one file or many? The RAG literature suggests 400–1,000 token sections, but this applies to chunk retrieval, not filesystem navigation where the agent decides which files to open. The coding agent literature suggests that one file per logical unit (analogous to one file per class) works well, but this hasn't been tested for documentation.

**No longitudinal study tracks a small-team KB from flat to structured.** GitLab's handbook comes closest but started as a company-wide initiative with executive commitment, not a domain-specific knowledge base maintained by part-time contributors. Whether the same organic growth pattern works for a specialized technical KB serving both humans and agents is untested.

**The interaction between self-contained documents and knowledge graph construction is understudied.** The team wants documents to eventually support knowledge graph construction, but the decontextualization literature focuses on retrieval, not on extracting structured relationships from self-contained text. Whether self-contained documents are easier or harder to parse into a knowledge graph than atomic linked documents remains an open empirical question that the team will need to learn through direct experimentation.
